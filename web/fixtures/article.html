Small teams can build software faster than ever. But writing the first version is only one part of building a company. The work of finding customers, earning trust, and supporting the people who depend on the product does not disappear when the code becomes cheaper.

A smaller team can build the product. It still has to build the trust.

What the prototype cannot tell you

A prototype answers whether a particular interaction can work. It cannot tell you whether anyone will return, recommend it to a colleague, or trust it with a difficult task. Those questions need encounters with real people and their actual work.

The strongest early signal is often modest: one person finds something useful enough to share, and the recipient understands it without a guided tour. That exchange can teach a small team more than a room full of polite compliments.

Keep the context attached

A short quotation can make an argument easy to discuss. It can also leave out the conditions that made the argument reasonable. When a comment points back to the original passage, the next reader can inspect that context and decide for themselves.

Building less can be a strong choice. The important question is which complete experience remains when the extras are removed. A clear beginning, a useful result, and a reliable way to recover from an error are a better foundation than an impressive list of unfinished features.
